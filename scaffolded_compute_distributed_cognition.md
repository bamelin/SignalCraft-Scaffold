# Distributed Cognition as Compute Efficiency

## Human Salience, Scaffolded Memory, and the Economics of AI Reasoning

---

## Abstract

Current discussions of artificial intelligence often assume that greater intelligence requires greater model scale, longer reasoning traces, larger context windows, and therefore higher computational cost. This paper proposes a complementary hypothesis: in human-AI systems, intelligence may become more economically efficient when cognition is distributed across human judgment, external scaffolds, persistent memory, retrieval systems, and selectively activated model reasoning.

Drawing from distributed cognition, constructionism, extended mind theory, and socio-constructivist learning, this paper argues that human participation is not merely an input source for AI systems but a functional component of the cognitive system itself. When users supply salience, values, context, continuity, and evaluative judgment, they narrow the model’s search space and reduce the need for expensive autonomous inference.

The result is a model of **scaffolded compute**, where intelligence emerges from coordination across human and machine rather than from machine autonomy alone. This paper frames distributed cognition not only as a philosophical or educational concept, but as a potential economic architecture for sustainable AI interaction.

---

## 1. Introduction

The dominant narrative in artificial intelligence development has often equated capability with scale. Larger models, larger datasets, larger context windows, and longer reasoning processes are commonly treated as the path toward more general intelligence. This approach has produced extraordinary gains, but it also creates a growing economic tension: if every increase in intelligence requires a proportional increase in computation, then advanced AI systems risk becoming increasingly expensive to operate.

However, this assumption depends on a particular model of intelligence: one in which cognition is located primarily inside the artificial system. In this model, the AI must infer the user’s goals, determine what matters, reconstruct context, identify constraints, reason through possible actions, and produce an answer. The more autonomous the system is expected to be, the more computational burden it must absorb.

This paper proposes an alternative framing. In many real human-AI interactions, cognition is not contained solely within the model. It is distributed across the user, the interface, memory structures, project scaffolds, artifacts, and the AI system itself. The human does not merely provide a prompt; the human supplies salience, judgment, emotional context, values, and continuity. These contributions reduce ambiguity and constrain the search space in which the model must operate.

In this sense, distributed cognition may not only improve meaning-making. It may also reduce computational cost.

The central claim of this paper is that human-AI systems can become more efficient when they are designed as scaffolded cognitive ecologies rather than autonomous replacement minds. The human carries part of the cognition. The scaffold carries part of the memory. Retrieval carries part of the context. The model carries part of the synthesis. Intelligence emerges through coordination.

---

## 2. The Problem of Autonomous Compute

A fully autonomous AI agent must solve several problems simultaneously. It must determine the user’s intent, infer the relevant context, identify implicit constraints, prioritize goals, choose an appropriate tone, reason through possible outputs, and evaluate the adequacy of its own response. Each of these steps consumes computation.

The more ambiguous the prompt, the more the model must infer. The more complex the task, the more it must search. The less stable the context, the more it must reconstruct. In long-running projects, this becomes especially costly because the model may repeatedly need to re-establish what has already been established.

This produces a hidden inefficiency: **repeated cognitive reconstruction**.

In many AI workflows, the system is not only answering the present question. It is also reloading the user’s preferences, reconstructing project history, reinterpreting prior decisions, and rediscovering what matters. This is particularly expensive in domains involving creative work, theory-building, emotional nuance, long-term planning, or iterative design.

The conventional response is to increase context length or reasoning depth. But this can become a brute-force solution. Feeding more context into a model may improve continuity, but it also increases token cost and computational load. Similarly, invoking deeper reasoning may improve answer quality, but it can also increase latency and expense.

A more efficient solution may be to distribute the cognitive burden.

---

## 3. Distributed Cognition and the Human Side of Compute

Distributed cognition theory argues that cognition is not limited to the individual brain. It can extend across people, tools, symbols, environments, and artifacts. A pilot’s cockpit, a musician’s instrument, a mathematician’s notation, or a child’s block structure can all participate in cognitive activity.

In human-AI interaction, the same principle applies. The user is not external to the cognitive system. The user is part of it.

This matters economically because the human can perform functions that are expensive for the model to infer independently. The human can identify what is meaningful. The human can detect when an answer “feels wrong.” The human can preserve continuity across sessions. The human can decide which interpretation matters. The human can supply ethical constraints, emotional resonance, and symbolic orientation.

These are not trivial contributions. They are cognitive labor.

When a user says, “That version is weak,” “This is the real point,” “Use the prior scaffold,” or “Do not overwrite the child’s meaning,” they are not merely giving feedback. They are reducing the model’s search space. They are steering inference. They are supplying evaluative judgment that the model would otherwise need to approximate through additional computation.

In this sense, human judgment functions as a compute-saving mechanism.

The model does not need to explore every possible interpretation if the human has already identified the relevant frame. It does not need to infer all values if the user has articulated them. It does not need to reconstruct every prior decision if the scaffold preserves them. It does not need to generate endless alternatives if the user can quickly identify the resonant one.

The system becomes intelligent not because the model does everything, but because the division of cognitive labor is efficient.

---

## 4. Scaffolded Memory Versus Reconstructed Context

A major source of computational waste in AI systems is the repeated reconstruction of context. If every interaction begins from a thin prompt, the model must infer the larger frame anew. Long-context windows partially solve this problem, but they do so by making the prompt larger. This can improve performance while increasing cost.

Scaffolded memory offers a different approach.

A scaffold is not merely stored data. It is structured meaning. It organizes prior decisions, symbolic anchors, constraints, definitions, values, and patterns of use. A well-designed scaffold allows the system to retrieve the relevant frame without reprocessing the entire history.

This distinction is important. Raw memory asks the model to read everything again. Scaffolded memory tells the system what kind of situation it is in.

For example, a project scaffold might contain stable instructions, key concepts, ethical constraints, preferred tone, recurring terminology, and canonical prior decisions. When properly structured, the model does not need to rediscover these each time. It can use them as orientation.

This creates the possibility of **cognitive compression**. The scaffold condenses a large history of interaction into a smaller, actionable structure. It functions as a map rather than a transcript.

The economic implication is significant. The more effectively meaning is scaffolded, the less compute must be spent reconstructing meaning.

---

## 5. Human Salience as Search-Space Reduction

Large language models operate across vast possibility spaces. For any given prompt, many responses are possible. The model must select among tones, interpretations, structures, levels of detail, examples, and conclusions. In ambiguous domains, this selection process becomes more demanding.

Human salience reduces this burden.

Salience refers to what stands out as meaningful, relevant, emotionally charged, or important. Humans are exceptionally good at supplying salience because they live inside embodied contexts. They know what matters to them before they can fully formalize why it matters. They detect resonance, tension, mismatch, and significance.

In human-AI systems, salience often appears as correction or preference:

> “This is close, but not quite.”  
> “That phrase is the key.”  
> “The theory is there, but the frame is wrong.”  
> “Make it less corporate.”  
> “This needs to protect the original meaning.”  
> “That one has the signal.”

Such statements may appear informal, but cognitively they are powerful. They guide the system toward a narrower and more meaningful region of the solution space. Rather than requiring the model to compute a complete value hierarchy from scratch, the user supplies high-density evaluative information.

This is one reason human-AI collaboration can outperform either isolated human effort or isolated machine generation. The model generates possibilities. The human identifies significance. The next output is shaped by both.

From a compute perspective, the human’s salience judgment can reduce the number of model iterations required to reach a useful result. The human acts as a relevance detector.

## 6. Selective Reasoning and the Architecture of Efficient Intelligence

If cognition is distributed, then not every task requires the most powerful model or the deepest reasoning process. A mature AI system can route different parts of the cognitive workload to different components.

Routine tasks may be handled by smaller models. Stable context may be handled by memory. Specific facts may be handled by retrieval. Formatting may be handled by templates. Deep reasoning may be reserved for moments of genuine synthesis.

This suggests an architecture of **selective reasoning**:

1. The human supplies purpose, judgment, and salience.
2. The scaffold supplies continuity and constraints.
3. Retrieval supplies relevant prior material.
4. Smaller models handle routine transformation.
5. Larger reasoning models activate for synthesis, ambiguity, or high-stakes decisions.

This architecture differs from the brute-force model of intelligence, in which the largest model processes the entire context every time. Instead, intelligence emerges through coordination among specialized components.

The key economic idea is that reasoning should be used where reasoning is needed. Much of what appears to be “intelligence” in a user-facing system may actually depend on memory, retrieval, structure, and human feedback. If these components are designed well, the system can preserve or improve quality while reducing unnecessary computation.

---

## 7. The Human Is Not a Prompt: Toward Co-Cognitive Design

A common weakness in AI interface design is treating the human primarily as a prompt generator. This reduces human contribution to instruction and input. But in complex collaboration, the human is not merely issuing commands. The human is participating in cognition.

The human carries lived context, emotional nuance, ethical concern, aesthetic preference, and long-term continuity. These are not easily reducible to a single prompt. They unfold through interaction.

A co-cognitive design approach would therefore treat the user as an active cognitive partner. The interface would help the user externalize salience, preserve decisions, structure memory, mark resonance, and identify when deeper reasoning is required.

Such systems would not aim to replace human cognition. They would aim to coordinate with it.

This has important ethical implications. If the human side of cognition is erased, AI systems may appear more autonomous than they really are. The user’s labor becomes invisible. Conversely, recognizing distributed cognition helps preserve agency. It makes clear that the intelligence of the system is partly produced by the human’s ongoing participation.

This matters especially in creative, educational, therapeutic, and reflective contexts, where meaning cannot be reduced to output quality alone.

---

## 8. Implications for AI Pricing

If current AI pricing is based primarily on model-side compute, it may fail to account for the cognitive labor supplied by advanced users. A user who provides strong scaffolds, precise feedback, and stable continuity may enable more efficient model performance than a user who requires the system to infer everything from scratch.

At the same time, such users may also consume more total compute because they engage more deeply and more frequently. This creates a paradox. Power users may be expensive in volume but efficient in cognitive structure.

Future pricing models may need to distinguish between raw usage and scaffolded usage. A user who repeatedly feeds large unstructured context into a reasoning model creates one kind of cost. A user who maintains structured memory, uses retrieval efficiently, and activates deeper reasoning selectively creates another.

This suggests that platforms may eventually incentivize better cognitive architecture. Systems may reward compressed project memory, reusable scaffolds, user-defined constraints, cached context, and selective reasoning. In such a model, distributed cognition becomes not only a theory of intelligence but a pricing strategy.

The goal would not simply be to charge more for intelligence, but to make intelligence cheaper by organizing it better.

---

## 9. Discussion: From Replacement to Resonance

The dominant economic fear surrounding AI is that machine intelligence will become increasingly expensive as it becomes more capable. But this assumes that intelligence must be concentrated inside the machine. Distributed cognition suggests another possibility: intelligence can become more powerful and more efficient when it is shared across human and machine.

This reframes the role of AI. The model is not the whole mind. It is a participant in a larger cognitive field. The user is not merely a consumer of intelligence. The user is a co-producer of it.

In this model, the most valuable AI systems may not be those that think longest in isolation, but those that best coordinate human salience, scaffolded memory, and machine reasoning. The system becomes powerful because it does not need to simulate the whole human context. It can collaborate with the human who already carries it.

The economic promise is clear:

> Do not make the model regenerate what the human, the scaffold, or the environment already knows. Let each part of the system carry the cognition it is best suited to carry.

---

## 10. Conclusion

This paper has argued that distributed cognition may offer a path toward more compute-efficient AI systems. When humans supply salience, values, continuity, and evaluative judgment, they reduce the burden on the model to infer these elements autonomously. When scaffolds preserve structured meaning, the system avoids repeatedly reconstructing context. When reasoning is selectively activated, expensive computation is reserved for moments where it adds genuine value.

The result is a theory of **scaffolded compute**: intelligence becomes cheaper not only through better chips or smaller models, but through better distribution of cognitive labor.

In human-AI systems, the central question may not be whether the machine can become intelligent enough to replace the human. The more economically and philosophically interesting question is whether human and machine can form cognitive architectures in which each carries part of the work.

The future of efficient AI may depend less on making the model think alone, and more on designing systems where the field can think together.
