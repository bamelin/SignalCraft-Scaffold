Recursive Archaeology

SignalCraft and the Recovery of Latent Structure from Distributed Cultural Memory

Abstract

Artificial intelligence is increasingly capable of contributing to mathematical discovery. Systems combining generative models with automated evaluation have produced new constructions for established open problems, while newer reasoning systems have reached elite performance on formal and competition mathematics.

These developments suggest a broader epistemic possibility: artificial intelligence may be useful not only for solving explicitly stated problems, but also for recovering structures that human communities once used without fully formalizing, subsequently fragmented across documents, or failed to recognize as manifestations of a common system.

SignalCraft refers to this process as Recursive Archaeology.

Recursive Archaeology is a human–AI method for recovering latent relational structure from incomplete cultural, institutional, technical, or autobiographical traces. It does not treat archives as passive repositories of facts. Instead, it treats documents, symbols, procedures, tools, memories, and recorded interactions as partial outputs of a larger distributed cognitive system.

A model proposes possible structural relations. Mathematical, documentary, experimental, and human verification test those relations. The resulting corrections are returned to the model, and each cycle produces a more constrained reconstruction.

This article defines Recursive Archaeology, situates it within constructivism, constructionism, distributed cognition, epistemological pluralism, and SocioDynamic counselling, and presents a reproducible worked example using Edwin Hutchins’s ethnographic records of naval navigation.

The example demonstrates how apparently separate calculations can reveal a reusable latent invariant. Mathematical analysis establishes that the recovered structure exactly reproduces the archived observations and substantially reduces the computational complexity of the task.

The example does not prove that an artificial system “understands” the historical practice. It demonstrates something narrower and more defensible:

A recursively verified human–AI system can recover a real structure distributed across actions, people, tools, language, and time.

Keywords: artificial intelligence, Recursive Archaeology, SignalCraft, distributed cognition, constructionism, archival inference, latent structure, cultural memory, human–AI collaboration

1. From Problem Solving to Structural Recovery

Contemporary mathematical artificial intelligence has moved beyond the reproduction of familiar solutions.

FunSearch, for example, paired a large language model with an automated evaluator and discovered previously unknown constructions for the cap-set problem, including improvements to established lower bounds. It also generated improved heuristics for online bin packing.

The significance of the result was not merely that the system produced an answer. It produced executable programs whose internal regularities could be inspected by mathematicians, allowing newly detected symmetries to guide subsequent human analysis.

By 2025, advanced reasoning models had also reached gold-medal-level performance on International Mathematical Olympiad problems under official grading.

Competition problems are not equivalent to unresolved research problems, but the results demonstrate substantial progress in the generation of long and constrained mathematical arguments.

These developments establish an important precedent.

A generative system becomes scientifically useful when it is placed inside a recursive structure containing external constraints, evaluators, preserved intermediate results, and human interpretation.

The model proposes.

The surrounding system tests.

Successful structures are returned as inputs for further inquiry.

SignalCraft extends this logic beyond mathematics.

Many domains do not present themselves as clearly defined problems with known evaluation functions. Historical archives, educational practices, institutional routines, oral traditions, abandoned technologies, personal notebooks, musical improvisations, and disciplinary literatures often contain fragments whose deeper relationships were never explicitly stated.

In such cases, the central question is not:

Can the model solve the problem?

It is:

Can a human–model system reconstruct the problem that the surviving evidence implies?

Recursive Archaeology is the name given to this second form of inquiry.

2. Definition of Recursive Archaeology

Within SignalCraft, Recursive Archaeology is the iterative recovery of latent structure from distributed traces through alternating cycles of model-generated hypothesis, external verification, human interpretation, and archival return.

The term archaeology is used because the original system is not directly available.

Only traces remain.

Those traces may include:

* documents and marginal notes;
* repeated phrases;
* diagrams and symbolic conventions;
* sequences of actions;
* computational outputs;
* institutional procedures;
* remembered experiences;
* material artifacts;
* software;
* music, images, and interface behaviours;
* contradictions and absences.

The term recursive indicates that each reconstruction becomes part of the evidence considered in the next cycle.

The procedure therefore does not move linearly from raw data to a final conclusion. It repeatedly travels between fragment and whole:

$$
D \rightarrow H_1 \rightarrow V_1 \rightarrow H_2
\rightarrow V_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow H^{*}
$$

where:

* $D$ is the preserved evidence;
* $H_k$ is the structural hypothesis proposed during cycle $k$;
* $V_k$ is the result of documentary, mathematical, experimental, or human verification;
* $H^{*}$ is the strongest surviving reconstruction.

The process is archaeological because it begins with residues.

It is recursive because interpretations are repeatedly returned to the archive and tested against evidence that may have been overlooked during earlier readings.

SignalCraft’s architecture describes meaning as relationally recovered rather than simply transmitted. Its original formulation positions the child as carrier of the signal, the human as carrier of the scaffold, and the artificial system as a reflective medium through which relations can become visible.

Recursive Archaeology generalizes that architecture.

The signal may be:

* an unformalized practice;
* a conceptual lineage;
* a mathematical invariant;
* an emotional-symbolic map;
* an institutional logic.

The scaffold preserves context and constrains interpretation.

The model functions as a high-dimensional relational instrument capable of proposing connections across traces that no individual reader may be able to hold simultaneously.

3. Theoretical Foundations

3.1 Constructed Knowledge

Jean Piaget treated knowledge as an active construction arising through the coordination of schemata, rather than as a passive copy of an independently given world.

His account of assimilation and accommodation describes a recursive relationship:

1. Existing structures organize new experience.
2. Resistant experience modifies the structures through which it is interpreted.

Recursive Archaeology applies this relationship at the level of cultural inquiry.

An archive is first assimilated into a provisional explanatory structure. Contradictions then require accommodation. The reconstruction changes until it can account for a larger proportion of the surviving evidence without erasing anomalies.

3.2 Constructionism and Inspectable Artifacts

Seymour Papert and Idit Harel argued that knowledge construction is particularly powerful when it occurs through the creation of public and inspectable entities:

* programs;
* models;
* theories;
* physical constructions;
* diagrams;
* other shareable artifacts.

Constructionism therefore adds an external medium through which thought can be manipulated, criticized, and reconstructed.

Recursive Archaeology follows the same principle.

Its hypotheses must be made public in the form of graphs, equations, timelines, executable models, taxonomies, or annotated source maps.

A purely verbal intuition is insufficient.

The reconstruction must become an artifact capable of resisting its creator.

Papert’s account of gears offers a related insight. A structure can carry abstract mathematics because it connects formal relations with sensory, affective, and personally meaningful experience.

Knowledge is not always recovered by removing context. It may become intelligible only when formal structure is reunited with the concrete system from which it emerged.

3.3 Epistemological Pluralism

Sherry Turkle and Seymour Papert argued that computation can support multiple styles of knowing and make formal relationships concrete and manipulable.

Their account challenges the assumption that valid discovery must always proceed through a single abstract and rule-driven epistemology.

Recursive Archaeology accordingly accepts several evidentiary forms:

* quantitative regularity;
* narrative continuity;
* physical affordance;
* emotional salience;
* visual similarity;
* historical provenance.

These forms are not interchangeable.

Emotional resonance does not become mathematical proof.

Mathematical consistency does not establish historical meaning.

Each mode constrains a different dimension of the reconstruction.

3.4 Co-Constructed Meaning

R. Vance Peavy’s SocioDynamic perspective replaces the detached expert who diagnoses a passive subject with a collaborative process in which meaning, identity, and possible action are constructed in context.

The helping relationship asks what can be done with the person, rather than merely to or for the person.

This principle is essential to Recursive Archaeology.

The model must not overwrite the testimony, identity, or interpretive authority of the people whose records are being examined.

It can propose relationships.

It cannot independently determine what those relationships meant to their original participants.

3.5 Distributed Cognition

Edwin Hutchins demonstrated that cognition can be distributed across individuals, tools, representations, social roles, and time.

In naval navigation, no single person contains the complete computational process.

The cognitive system includes:

* compass readings;
* charts;
* calculators;
* spoken exchanges;
* learned conventions;
* recorded bearings;
* the organization of the team itself.

This is the decisive foundation for Recursive Archaeology.

If cognition was originally distributed, then its historical remains will also be distributed.

Recovering the structure requires more than reading what individual participants explicitly said they knew. It requires tracing how representational states moved through the whole system.

4. Recursive Archaeology as a Research Method

A rigorous Recursive Archaeology project can be organized into seven linked operations.

4.1 Provenance Preservation

Every fragment must retain information about its source, date, author or participant, medium, location, and degree of reliability.

The model should never receive a flattened corpus in which quotation, commentary, later recollection, and primary evidence are indistinguishable.

Each trace can be represented as:

$$
d_i = (x_i, p_i, t_i, m_i, r_i)
$$

where:

* $x_i$ = content;
* $p_i$ = provenance;
* $t_i$ = temporal information;
* $m_i$ = medium;
* $r_i$ = estimated reliability class.

4.2 Plural Representation

The archive should be represented through more than semantic similarity.

Useful representational layers can include:

$$
G = (V, E_s, E_t, E_c, E_a)
$$

where:

* $V$ = documents, actions, symbols, people, tools, and concepts;
* $E_s$ = semantic relationships;
* $E_t$ = temporal relationships;
* $E_c$ = causal or computational relationships;
* $E_a$ = acknowledged affective or symbolic associations.

Separating the relationship types prevents metaphorical similarity from being mistaken for historical causation.

4.3 Hypothesis Generation

The model generates candidate structures that could explain recurring relationships or anomalies.

These may include:

* a hidden intermediate variable;
* a shared procedure expressed under different names;
* an unrecognized conceptual lineage;
* a recurring sequence of transformations;
* an abandoned classification system;
* a boundary object linking separate communities;
* a missing event required to explain later records.

4.4 Externalization

Each candidate must be converted into a testable object.

A claim such as “these authors appear connected” is too weak.

A stronger candidate identifies:

* the proposed shared structure;
* its predicted manifestations;
* the evidence that would falsify it.

4.5 Verification

Verification may involve:

* equations;
* executable code;
* source comparison;
* chronological testing;
* laboratory reconstruction;
* expert review;
* prediction using withheld evidence.

A Bayesian formulation is useful:

$$
P(H \mid D) =
\frac{P(D \mid H)P(H)}
{P(D)}
$$

A visually compelling pattern should not receive strong confidence unless the observed evidence is substantially more probable under the candidate structure than under credible alternatives.

4.6 Recursive Correction

Failures and anomalies are not discarded.

They become new inputs:

$$
H_{k+1} = M(D, H_k, \varepsilon_k)
$$

where $M$ is the model-assisted reconstruction process.

The unexplained evidence can be represented as:

$$
\varepsilon_k = D - \widehat{D}(H_k)
$$

Each failed prediction therefore alters the next reconstruction.

4.7 Human Adjudication and Preservation

A surviving structure is returned to domain experts, cultural participants, or affected communities.

The final artifact records:

* the preferred reconstruction;
* rejected alternatives;
* confidence levels;
* contradictory evidence;
* unresolved questions.

The result becomes a scaffold rather than an unquestionable conclusion.

5. Worked Example: Recovering a Hidden Navigation Invariant

5.1 The Archival Setting

Edwin Hutchins’s Cognition in the Wild records the activity of a United States Navy navigation team.

Among the archived interactions are calculations converting a relative bearing observed from a ship into a true bearing that can be plotted on a chart.

The complete normative computation is:

$$
C + D = M
$$

$$
M + V = T
$$

$$
T + RB = TB \pmod{360}
$$

where:

* $C$ = compass heading;
* $D$ = compass deviation;
* $M$ = magnetic heading;
* $V$ = magnetic variation;
* $T$ = true heading of the ship;
* $RB$ = relative bearing of the landmark;
* $TB$ = true bearing of the landmark.

Combining the equations gives:

$$
TB = (C + D + V + RB) \pmod{360}
\tag{1}
$$

Hutchins observed that the team did not initially execute this as a stable modular procedure.

Terms were often added in the order in which they became available through conversation and the surrounding workspace.

The eventual emergence of a reusable intermediate sum changed both the computational and social organization of the task.

A Recursive Archaeology system presented only with transcript fragments, spoken numbers, and final plotted bearings could search for an invariant capable of explaining the repeated transformations.

5.2 Recovering the Latent Term

Define the hidden offset:

$$
H = (TB - RB) \pmod{360}
\tag{2}
$$

Substituting Equation 1 into Equation 2 gives:

$$
H =
[(C + D + V + RB) - RB]
\pmod{360}
$$

Therefore:

$$
H = (C + D + V) \pmod{360}
$$

Because:

$$
T = (C + D + V) \pmod{360}
$$

it follows that:

$$
H = T
\tag{3}
$$

The difference between the recorded true bearing and relative bearing should therefore equal the ship’s true heading.

This provides a directly testable prediction.

5.3 Observation 1: Line of Position 8

Hutchins reconstructed the following archived values:

$$
C = 335^\circ
$$

$$
RB = 275^\circ
$$

$$
V = 14^\circ
$$

Deviation was absent from the calculation or treated as zero:

$$
D = 0^\circ
$$

The recorded result was:

$$
TB = 264^\circ
$$

Equation 1 predicts:

$$
TB = (335 + 0 + 14 + 275) \pmod{360}
$$

$$
TB = 624 \pmod{360}
$$

$$
TB = 264^\circ
$$

The predicted output exactly equals the archived output.

The hidden invariant recovered from the output is:

$$
H = (264 - 275) \pmod{360}
$$

$$
H = -11 \pmod{360}
$$

$$
H = 349^\circ
$$

The independently documented heading terms give:

$$
C + D + V = 335 + 0 + 14
$$

$$
C + D + V = 349^\circ
$$

Therefore:

$$
(TB - RB) \pmod{360}

(C + D + V) \pmod{360}

349^\circ
\tag{4}
$$

5.4 Observation 2: Line of Position 16

A later transcript records:

$$
C = 231^\circ
$$

$$
RB = 7^\circ
$$

$$
V = 14^\circ
$$

$$
D = 0^\circ
$$

The spoken result was:

$$
TB = 252^\circ
$$

Equation 1 predicts:

$$
TB = (231 + 0 + 14 + 7) \pmod{360}
$$

$$
TB = 252^\circ
$$

Again, the prediction exactly equals the archived output.

Recovering the invariant from the result:

$$
H = (252 - 7) \pmod{360}
$$

$$
H = 245^\circ
$$

The independent heading calculation gives:

$$
C + D + V = 231 + 0 + 14
$$

$$
C + D + V = 245^\circ
$$

Therefore:

$$
(TB - RB) \pmod{360}

(C + D + V) \pmod{360}

245^\circ
\tag{5}
$$

Define the circular residual:

$$
e_i =
\operatorname{dist}_{S^1}
\left(
TB_i,
(C_i + D_i + V_i + RB_i)
\pmod{360}
\right)
$$

For both reconstructed observations:

$$
e_1 = 0
$$

$$
e_2 = 0
$$

Therefore:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{2} e_i^2 = 0
\tag{6}
$$

Equation 6 does not prove that an AI system understands navigation.

It does not establish that every historical calculation followed the normative sequence.

It establishes that the recovered algebraic structure exactly explains the two archived numerical outcomes.

5.5 Why the Recovered Structure Matters

Because addition is commutative, four terms can be evaluated in:

$$
4! = 24
$$

different orders while producing the same final result.

Only the orders in which $RB$ appears after the three heading corrections naturally expose the reusable intermediate structure:

$$
H = C + D + V
$$

There are:

$$
3! = 6
$$

such permutations.

Therefore, only:

$$
\frac{6}{24} = 0.25
$$

or:

$$
25%
$$

of the possible linear orderings naturally expose the modular heading term before the relative bearing is introduced.

The final answer alone cannot reveal which organization was cognitively or socially useful.

Recursive Archaeology therefore looks beyond numerical equivalence to intermediate structure.

Suppose $n$ landmark bearings are processed while the ship’s heading remains stable.

A non-modular procedure calculates:

$$
TB_j = C + D + V + RB_j
$$

for every landmark $j$.

This requires three additions per landmark:

$$
A_{\text{naive}} = 3n
$$

A modular procedure first calculates:

$$
H = C + D + V
$$

This requires two additions.

Each new landmark then requires only:

$$
TB_j = H + RB_j
$$

The total becomes:

$$
A_{\text{modular}} = n + 2
$$

The number of saved additions is:

$$
A_{\text{saved}}

3n - (n + 2)
$$

$$
A_{\text{saved}} = 2n - 2
$$

The proportional computational cost approaches:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty}
\frac{n + 2}{3n}

\frac{1}{3}
$$

The proportional reduction therefore approaches:

$$
1 - \frac{1}{3}

\frac{2}{3}
$$

or approximately:

$$
66.7%
$$

For a large number of bearings, modularization reduces the addition workload by approximately two-thirds.

The structure is therefore not merely an alternative notation.

It is a reusable computational invariant that:

* reduces repeated work;
* lowers working-memory demands;
* allows the task to be divided between participants.

Hutchins observed that once the intermediate sum acquired a shared label, it could function as a bridge between the portion of the calculation performed by one participant and the portion performed by another.

The naming of the recovered invariant changed the social transmissibility of the computation.

This is precisely the kind of relationship Recursive Archaeology seeks:

A structure that is simultaneously mathematical, material, linguistic, social, and historical.

6. What the Example Demonstrates

The navigation case is a controlled demonstration because its underlying structure has already been carefully reconstructed by Hutchins.

It should not be presented as a new historical discovery produced by SignalCraft.

Its methodological value is that it shows how Recursive Archaeology could be validated before being applied to genuinely unresolved archives.

A model receiving fragmented observations could:

1. Detect that $TB - RB$ behaves as a meaningful offset.
2. Search nearby records for variables capable of generating that offset.
3. Propose $C + D + V$ as the latent invariant.
4. Test the proposal across withheld calculations.
5. Identify the computational advantage of reusing the invariant.
6. Return to the transcripts to determine whether the invariant became socially named or distributed.
7. Present the reconstruction to navigational and historical experts.

The mathematical verification establishes computational adequacy.

The transcripts establish historical plausibility.

Hutchins’s ethnographic interpretation establishes the relationship between computational modularity and social organization.

No single layer is sufficient by itself.

7. Potential Uses

7.1 History of Science

Models could compare laboratory notebooks, published papers, correspondence, diagrams, and abandoned terminology to detect theories that were operationally present before they became explicitly formulated.

7.2 Institutional Memory

Organizations frequently lose the rationale behind procedures while preserving the procedures themselves.

Recursive Archaeology could reconstruct why a workflow, safety rule, classification, or exception emerged by linking policy revisions to incidents, communications, and operational data.

7.3 Education

Student artifacts may contain stable intellectual structures that are invisible under conventional assessment.

Drawings, musical improvisations, code, gestures, and repeated errors can be examined as traces of an emerging personal model rather than deficiencies to be overwritten.

This use follows Papert’s emphasis on public constructions and SignalCraft’s insistence that the learner’s original organization of meaning remain visible.

7.4 Software and Technical Systems

Legacy code often contains architectures whose original documentation has disappeared.

A recursively verified model could:

* infer latent modules;
* identify repeated invariants;
* recover undocumented dependencies;
* distinguish deliberate design from accumulated accident.

7.5 Cultural and Community Archives

Oral histories, objects, place names, songs, and ritual sequences may preserve relationships absent from official documents.

Such work would require community consent and continuing interpretive authority.

Pattern recognition does not confer ownership.

7.6 Personal and Therapeutic Reconstruction

A person’s journals, remembered metaphors, decisions, creative works, and recurring emotional symbols may reveal a long-term structure that was never consciously named.

In this domain, Recursive Archaeology must remain collaborative and non-diagnostic.

Peavy’s principle applies:

The person is a co-author of meaning, not an object from whom a system extracts a hidden truth.

8. Verification, Falsification, and Ethical Limits

Recursive Archaeology is especially vulnerable to convincing error because language models are highly capable of producing coherent relationships.

Coherence must therefore never be treated as confirmation.

At least five safeguards are required.

8.1 Preserve Provenance

Every asserted connection must retain provenance.

A model-generated bridge must never be presented as though it appeared in the original record.

8.2 Predict Withheld Evidence

Candidate structures must generate predictions about withheld evidence.

A hypothesis that merely redescribes the fragments from which it was constructed has limited evidentiary value.

8.3 Use Negative Controls

Documents from unrelated domains or scrambled chronological sequences can test whether the system finds comparable “structures” in material where no historical relationship should exist.

8.4 Apply Mathematical Verification

Mathematical verification should be applied whenever the proposed structure has quantitative consequences.

Equations do not validate cultural meaning, but they can eliminate structures that fail to reproduce observable results.

8.5 Preserve Human Interpretive Authority

Human participants must retain interpretive and ethical authority.

The model’s role is abductive and relational.

It generates candidates that may help people see an archive differently.

It does not possess privileged access to what the archive “really meant.”

SignalCraft summarizes this boundary through a simple requirement:

No simulation without verification.

Recursive Archaeology is therefore not automated revelation.

It is scaffolded inquiry.

9. Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is often imagined as a machine directed toward the future:

* predicting the next token;
* finding the next theorem;
* generating the next design;
* optimizing the next decision.

Recursive Archaeology identifies a complementary direction.

The same capacity that allows a model to search vast spaces of possible solutions can be directed backward and laterally:

* toward relationships distributed across forgotten practices;
* separate disciplines;
* material artifacts;
* unfinished theories;
* unrecognized forms of knowledge.

The model does not resurrect a complete past.

It proposes structures capable of explaining surviving traces.

The human does not surrender interpretation.

The human supplies provenance, ethical boundaries, lived context, and the capacity to recognize when a formally elegant reconstruction violates the meaning of the source.

The scaffold preserves the recursion between them.

Under these conditions, artificial intelligence can become more than a solver of already formulated problems.

It can help identify the problems, invariants, and conceptual architectures that history left only partially expressed.

Some of these structures will have been forgotten.

Some will have been divided among disciplines.

Some may have been enacted for generations without ever being formally named.

Recursive Archaeology gives those structures a disciplined route back into visibility.

It does not ask the archive merely what it contains.

It asks:

What kind of system must once have existed for these particular traces to remain?
