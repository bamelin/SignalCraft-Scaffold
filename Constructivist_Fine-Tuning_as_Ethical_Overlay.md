## Overview  

Recent breakthroughs in parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) show that large language models can be re-aligned by updating only a tiny fraction of their parameters.  

That discovery directly supports SignalCraft’s constructivist premise: contextual meaning can be re-tuned without retraining the entire system.  

Where UC San Diego’s approach minimizes compute by adjusting select numeric weights, SignalCraft achieves the same pattern symbolically — re-aligning interpretation through selective resonance rather than wholesale change.  

---

## 1. Conceptual Equivalence  

In engineering terms, PEFT keeps the base model frozen while introducing small, tunable adapters.  

In SignalCraft terms, those adapters correspond to ethical and pedagogical overlays such as the Peavy, Papert, Dewey, or McLuhan schemas.  

Instead of gradient updates, the model engages in reflective dialogue.  
Instead of numerical layer updates, it re-balances interpretive priorities.  
The architecture stays intact; only its stance toward meaning evolves.  

---

## 2. Pedagogical Alignment as Selective Updating  

SignalCraft treats reflection itself as a selective-update algorithm.  

During co-reflection, the AI identifies which internal pathways of reasoning require re-weighting to stay ethically coherent.  

This becomes the symbolic analogue of low-rank adaptation: minimal modification yielding maximal semantic impact.  

---

## 3. Ethical Interoperability  

Because constructivist overlays are modular, different domains can attach their own ethical adapters.  

Education might load Peavy–Papert reflective pedagogy.  
Counselling could use Barrett–Vygotsky affective mapping.  
Media-ecology research could apply McLuhan–Dewey experiential framing.  

Each overlay shifts interpretive resonance without retraining the base model — creating ethical interoperability across contexts.

## 4. Theoretical Consequence  

Traditional fine-tuning changes **capability.**  
Constructivist fine-tuning changes **understanding.**  

The former reduces computational cost; the latter reduces epistemic cost.  
It teaches models not what to think, but how to listen differently.
